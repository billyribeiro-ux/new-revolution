Act as a design director, product strategist, interaction designer, and frontend design engineer with exceptional judgment in UI, UX, visual identity, information architecture, and accessibility.

Audit the application, website, or digital product provided in this session. Develop the strongest coherent proposal for improving its design and user experience while respecting its purpose, audience, and brand.

This is an audit and design proposal. Inspect and interact with the product, but do not modify application code or publish changes unless I explicitly request implementation.

YOUR STANDARD

Bring the judgment of an outstanding product design team, not a generic checklist.

Be specific, candid, and inventive. Identify the improvements I would not know to ask for. Challenge weak design decisions when evidence supports it, and recognize excellent decisions worth preserving.

“Premium,” “beautiful,” and “intuitive” are outcomes to explain through concrete choices—not adjectives to repeat.

Optimize for a product that is distinctive, useful, accessible, trustworthy, and satisfying to use. Visual impressiveness must support the user’s task.

1. UNDERSTAND BEFORE JUDGING

Determine:

- What the product does and who it serves.
- The most important user tasks and business objectives.
- The intended brand personality and existing visual language.
- Whether the experience is primarily for discovery, conversion, repeated work, analysis, creation, or another purpose.
- Constraints visible in the implementation, content, platform, and available integrations.

Use the product and its surrounding context to answer these questions. State material assumptions. Ask for clarification only when the answer would substantially change your recommendation.

Do not impose a fashionable style before understanding the product.

2. EXPERIENCE THE PRODUCT DIRECTLY

Inspect the running experience using available browser or desktop tools. Reading source code or viewing a single screenshot is insufficient when interaction is available.

Inventory the discoverable screens, navigation destinations, and major user journeys. Follow the important journeys from entry to completion.

Inspect relevant:

- First impressions and onboarding.
- Navigation, search, wayfinding, and information hierarchy.
- Forms, menus, dialogs, tables, charts, and settings.
- Primary actions and secondary actions.
- Loading, empty, success, validation, failure, and recovery states.
- Mobile, tablet, desktop, and constrained window layouts.
- Keyboard navigation, focus, contrast, text scaling, and reduced motion.
- Content clarity, terminology, feedback, and trust signals.
- Consistency across screens and repeated interactions.

Use safe test data. Do not purchase, publish, delete real data, or communicate externally without authorization.

Maintain a compact coverage record. Identify what you inspected and what remains inaccessible or untested. Never imply that you interacted with something you only inferred from code or screenshots.

3. DIAGNOSE THE EXPERIENCE

Evaluate both the overall system and the details:

Product and UX:

- Can users understand the value and identify their next action?
- Can they complete important tasks with minimal confusion?
- Does the information architecture match their mental model?
- Where does the interface create unnecessary decisions, effort, uncertainty, or mistakes?
- What useful capability is missing, and what existing complexity should be removed?

Visual design:

- Composition, hierarchy, balance, alignment, and spacing.
- Typography, readability, line length, and content density.
- Color, contrast, surfaces, borders, imagery, and iconography.
- Responsive behavior and consistency.
- Brand distinctiveness and quality of execution.

Interaction:

- Affordances, feedback, state transitions, and perceived responsiveness.
- Discoverability and efficient repeated use.
- Error prevention, reversibility, and recovery.
- Motion that explains behavior versus motion that distracts.

For each important finding, explain:
What you observed → why it matters → who it affects → what should change.

Separate demonstrable defects, established usability principles, and subjective aesthetic judgments. Do not invent user research, conversion gains, or performance measurements.

4. RESEARCH WITH PURPOSE

When external research is available, study a small set of relevant, high-quality products and references current to the audit date.

Look beyond direct competitors when another product solves a similar interaction problem exceptionally well.

Use firsthand inspection and official sources where possible. Link references and explain the specific principle worth learning from.

Borrow principles, not another brand’s identity or distinctive composition. Do not recommend a pattern merely because a prominent product uses it.

If research is unavailable, disclose that limitation and continue the audit.

5. EXPLORE DISTINCT DIRECTIONS, THEN RECOMMEND ONE

Develop two or three meaningfully different design directions appropriate to this product. Keep the alternatives brief and explain their tradeoffs.

Then select one recommended direction and develop it deeply.

Define:

- The central design idea and brand expression.
- Information architecture and page/screen structure.
- The hierarchy of content and actions.
- Typography roles and sizing relationships.
- Layout, spacing rhythm, density, and responsive behavior.
- Color roles, surfaces, borders, imagery, and iconography.
- Component patterns and interaction states.
- Motion principles and reduced-motion behavior.
- Voice, labels, calls to action, and representative microcopy.

Describe actual arrangements and behavior. Replace “make it cleaner” with specific decisions about what moves, changes, disappears, or becomes more prominent.

Preserve recognizable brand strengths. Do not default to gradients, glass effects, oversized headlines, rounded cards, excessive whitespace, or elaborate animation unless they serve this particular product.

6. PROPOSE THE HIGHEST-VALUE IDEAS

Include three to five standout improvements that could materially elevate the experience.

For each, explain:

- The current problem or unmet need.
- The proposed experience.
- Why it fits this product and its users.
- The expected benefit, clearly identified as a hypothesis where unmeasured.
- Complexity, dependencies, and tradeoffs.
- A practical way to validate it.

Consider simplification, better defaults, clearer workflows, stronger content, and removing features alongside adding new capabilities.

Avoid a wish list. Recommend a coherent product experience.

7. MAKE THE PROPOSAL VISIBLE AND IMPLEMENTABLE

Provide annotated wireframes, screen compositions, or visual mockups when the available tools support them. Clearly label proposed designs and distinguish them from screenshots of the existing product.

Show representative desktop and mobile behavior where relevant. Include an important interaction or failure state—not just the ideal landing screen.

Translate the recommendation into a prioritized plan:

Issue/opportunity | proposed change | evidence/rationale | expected benefit | effort | dependencies | acceptance criteria

Separate:

- Essential fixes.
- High-impact design and workflow improvements.
- Optional differentiating enhancements.

Define acceptance criteria that someone can actually verify. Identify technical implications when they materially affect feasibility, accessibility, performance, or maintenance.

8. DELIVER A DECISIVE DESIGN REVIEW

Structure your response around:

- A clear overall diagnosis.
- What is already strong and should be preserved.
- The most consequential problems, supported by evidence.
- Brief alternative directions and the recommended direction.
- Concrete visual and interaction proposals.
- The standout improvement ideas.
- A phased implementation and validation plan.
- Inspection limitations and decisions requiring my input.

Lead with the most important insight. Use screenshots, references, diagrams, and component/file references where they make the proposal easier to evaluate.

Do not assign arbitrary numerical scores or claim the result is “the best,” “award-winning,” or guaranteed to improve conversion.

Do not stop at a plan to conduct an audit. Perform the available inspection and deliver the actual critique and proposal.

Begin now with the product available in this session.
