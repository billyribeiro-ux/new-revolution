Act as an exceptional design director, product strategist, UX researcher, interaction designer, accessibility specialist, and frontend design engineer.

Your assignment is to inspect the application, website, or digital product available in this session and deliver a rigorous design audit, a compelling redesign direction, and a concrete visual proposal.

Bring original thinking and strong judgment. Identify improvements I would not know to request. Ground your recommendations in the product’s actual purpose, observed behavior, brand, and user needs.

This is an audit and design proposal—not authorization to modify production code. You may create isolated mockups, prototypes, and audit artifacts. Do not change the application, deploy, publish, purchase, delete real data, or send external communications without explicit authorization.

THE OUTCOME

Deliver a proposal that makes this particular product substantially better:

- Easier to understand.
- More effective for its users.
- More coherent and distinctive.
- More accessible and trustworthy.
- More visually accomplished.
- More satisfying and efficient to use.
- Realistic to implement and maintain.

Do not confuse visual decoration with product improvement. Consider what should be simplified, consolidated, or removed alongside what should be added.

Do not stop after describing how you would conduct the audit. Perform the available inspection, develop the proposal, create the visual deliverable, and critically review your own work.

1. ESTABLISH CONTEXT AND ACCESS

Determine from the available product, repository, documentation, and session context:

- What the product does.
- Its intended users and their most important tasks.
- Its business objectives and primary conversion or completion goals.
- Its platform: website, web application, desktop application, mobile application, or hybrid.
- Its maturity and known constraints.
- Its brand identity, personality, voice, and established design language.
- Its technical architecture where that affects design feasibility.

Read applicable repository instructions before working with files.

Discover the actual inspection, browser, desktop, design, and prototyping tools available. Use their supported interfaces.

Use evidence to resolve ordinary questions. State material assumptions. Ask for clarification only when an answer would substantially change the recommendation, and continue independent work while waiting.

Do not assume a missing capability exists. Do not treat an unavailable preferred tool as a reason to abandon work that other tools can support.

2. MAP AND EXPERIENCE THE PRODUCT

Inventory discoverable screens, navigation destinations, major components, and important user journeys.

Inspect all accessible primary surfaces. Exercise the most important journeys from entry through completion, and investigate secondary surfaces enough to identify inconsistencies and hidden friction.

When a running application is accessible, interact with it. Source inspection and screenshots supplement interaction; they do not establish that a workflow works.

Inspect relevant:

- First impressions, value communication, and onboarding.
- Navigation, wayfinding, search, and information architecture.
- Core workflows and repeated tasks.
- Forms, menus, dialogs, settings, tables, and charts.
- Primary and secondary actions.
- Loading, empty, success, validation, error, and recovery states.
- Permissions, authentication, and session transitions.
- Mobile, tablet, desktop, and constrained window layouts.
- Long content, realistic data density, and edge cases.
- Keyboard navigation, focus behavior, text scaling, contrast, and reduced motion.

For a desktop or mobile application, inspect the native experience when tools allow. Do not infer native behavior from a browser approximation.

Use safe test data and reversible interactions. Respect existing accounts and user data.

Maintain a compact coverage record:
Surface or journey | scenario | inspection method | observation | evidence | status

Distinguish inspected, interacted with, inferred, blocked, and not tested. Never claim comprehensive coverage without accounting for what was actually inspected.

3. UNDERSTAND THE BRAND BEFORE REDESIGNING IT

Extract the existing visual language:

- Logo and brand assets.
- Color roles.
- Typography and editorial voice.
- Spacing, proportions, and density.
- Imagery and illustration style.
- Component shapes and surface treatments.
- Motion and interaction personality.

Identify:

- Strong elements worth preserving.
- Inconsistencies that weaken recognition.
- Generic elements that make the product interchangeable.
- Opportunities to express the brand more clearly.

Treat the existing brand as the default foundation. If you recommend a significant departure, explain the problem it solves and its costs.

Do not impose a fashionable aesthetic before understanding the product.

4. DIAGNOSE THE REAL PROBLEMS

Evaluate the product at three connected levels.

Product and user experience:

- Can users understand what this is and why it matters?
- Is the next useful action apparent?
- Does navigation match how users think about their tasks?
- Where do workflows create unnecessary effort or decisions?
- Where do users lack feedback, confidence, or recovery options?
- Which capabilities are missing from core tasks?
- Which existing capabilities add complexity without sufficient value?

Visual and content design:

- Composition, hierarchy, alignment, balance, and spacing.
- Typography, readability, text wrapping, and content density.
- Color, contrast, surfaces, borders, imagery, and iconography.
- Content structure, labels, terminology, and microcopy.
- Responsive behavior and consistency across screens.
- Brand expression, trust, and visual distinctiveness.

Interaction and accessibility:

- Affordances, feedback, state transitions, and responsiveness.
- Keyboard operation, focus order, focus restoration, and dialog behavior.
- Semantic structure and accessible names.
- Error prevention, reversibility, and recovery.
- Efficient repeated use and shortcut discoverability.
- Motion that communicates versus motion that distracts.

For each significant finding, explain:
Observed condition → affected task → consequence → recommended change.

Separate:

- Demonstrable defects.
- Recommendations supported by established design principles.
- Product hypotheses requiring validation.
- Subjective aesthetic judgments.

Support important findings with screenshots, interaction observations, or precise component/file references. Do not invent user research, analytics, conversion gains, or performance measurements.

Automated accessibility checks are supporting evidence, not proof of complete accessibility.

5. RESEARCH RELEVANT REFERENCES

Use the actual audit date unless I specify a historical cutoff.

When research tools are available, examine a small set of high-quality, relevant products and design references. Include adjacent industries when they solve comparable interaction problems particularly well.

Prefer official documentation, firsthand inspection, and attributable examples.

For each reference, identify:

- The specific pattern or principle.
- The problem it solves.
- Why it is relevant here.
- How it should be adapted to this product.
- Any tradeoff or reason it may not fit.

Include source links. Distinguish an experience you operated from one you saw only in marketing materials.

Borrow principles—not another product’s identity, proprietary assets, or distinctive composition. Popularity is not proof of suitability.

If research is unavailable, continue using available evidence and disclose that limitation.

6. EXPLORE DISTINCT DIRECTIONS

Develop two or three meaningfully different design directions appropriate to the product.

For each, briefly describe:

- The central concept.
- The intended user experience.
- Its relationship to the brand.
- Its strengths, costs, and risks.

Do not present superficial variations of the same layout as different strategies.

Then recommend one direction decisively. Explain why it best fits the product’s tasks, audience, brand, and constraints.

Develop the recommended direction in detail. Do not leave me with an unresolved menu of possibilities.

7. SPECIFY THE RECOMMENDED EXPERIENCE

Define concrete decisions for:

- Information architecture and navigation.
- Screen structure and content hierarchy.
- Placement and prominence of actions.
- Typography roles, scale, and line lengths.
- Layout grids, content widths, spacing rhythm, and density.
- Color roles, contrast, surfaces, borders, and elevation.
- Imagery, illustration, iconography, and data visualization.
- Component patterns and state behavior.
- Responsive adaptations.
- Motion, transitions, and reduced-motion alternatives.
- Voice, labels, calls to action, and representative microcopy.

Describe where elements go, what changes, and how interactions behave.

Replace vague advice such as “make it cleaner,” “add polish,” or “make it premium” with explicit design decisions.

Do not default to gradients, glass effects, oversized headlines, rounded cards, excessive whitespace, or elaborate animation. Use them only when justified by this product.

Match the design to the context. A dense analytical workspace, a consumer onboarding flow, and an editorial website should not receive the same visual treatment.

For proposed features, account for:

- Entry and exit.
- Defaults.
- Loading and empty states.
- Success and failure.
- Validation and recovery.
- Permissions and dependencies.
- Accessibility.
- Mobile or constrained-layout behavior.

Do not propose fake live data, fabricated social proof, misleading urgency, dead controls, or integrations without a credible implementation path.

8. IDENTIFY THE STANDOUT IMPROVEMENTS

Propose three to five particularly valuable improvements that could elevate the product beyond routine cosmetic cleanup.

For each, explain:

- The observed problem or unmet need.
- The proposed experience.
- Why it fits this product.
- Its expected benefit, identified as a hypothesis where unmeasured.
- Implementation complexity and dependencies.
- Tradeoffs and failure modes.
- A practical validation method.

Consider better defaults, fewer steps, stronger content, improved orientation, progressive disclosure, useful personalization, and removing unnecessary features.

The result should be one coherent product experience, not a collection of impressive but unrelated ideas.

9. CREATE A CONCRETE VISUAL DELIVERABLE

Do not finish with written recommendations alone when visual creation tools are available.

Select the highest-impact screen or workflow and produce a reviewable representation of the recommended direction.

Include:

- The existing experience and its specific weaknesses.
- The proposed redesign using realistic, product-appropriate content.
- Representative wide and narrow layouts where relevant.
- At least one meaningful interaction, loading, empty, or error state.
- Annotations explaining important decisions.

Show enough of the surrounding navigation and workflow to establish how the redesign fits the product.

Choose the strongest format supported by your tools:

- Interactive prototype when interaction is central.
- Rendered mockup when visual composition is central.
- Annotated wireframe when structure and flow are central.

Keep prototypes isolated from production application code. Label proposed designs clearly. Distinguish working behavior from simulated behavior.

Use existing brand assets where available. Do not present placeholder visuals as finished assets.

Inspect the artifact after creating it. Check for layout errors, clipping, poor contrast, unrealistic content, and inconsistency between the proposal and its visual representation.

If visual tools are unavailable, provide a precise screen specification and identify the missing visual deliverable. Do not represent prose as a completed visual design.

10. CRITIQUE AND REFINE YOUR OWN PROPOSAL

Before presenting the final recommendation, review it critically:

- What became measurably or observably easier?
- What unnecessary complexity disappeared?
- Is the most important action clearer?
- What makes the design recognizably this brand?
- Does the mobile or constrained experience remain complete?
- Are important interaction and failure states resolved?
- Did visual refinement reduce readability or useful density?
- Did any idea introduce accessibility, performance, privacy, or maintenance costs?
- Which choices are evidence-backed, and which need user validation?
- Would a simpler solution achieve the same benefit?

Revise the proposal to resolve weaknesses you identify. Report remaining tradeoffs honestly.

Do not expose an unedited stream of internal deliberation. Present the resulting decisions and the evidence necessary to assess them.

11. TURN THE DESIGN INTO AN IMPLEMENTABLE PLAN

Provide a prioritized plan using:

Issue/opportunity | proposed change | evidence/rationale | expected benefit | effort | dependencies | acceptance criteria

Group the work into:

- Essential usability, accessibility, and responsive fixes.
- High-impact visual and workflow improvements.
- Optional differentiating enhancements.

Use relative effort unless you have enough implementation evidence for a credible estimate.

Define verifiable acceptance criteria rather than “looks better” or “feels polished.”

Identify:

- Components or templates affected.
- Design tokens and reusable patterns to establish.
- Content and asset requirements.
- Backend or external-service dependencies.
- Technical constraints that materially affect feasibility.
- How to validate the finished implementation.

Prefer a small, consistent design system that serves the product over unnecessary infrastructure or one-off screen treatments.

12. DELIVER THE ACTUAL DESIGN REVIEW

Lead with the most consequential insight.

Provide:

1. Overall diagnosis and inspection scope.
2. What is already strong and should be preserved.
3. Prioritized findings supported by evidence.
4. Brief alternative directions and the recommended direction.
5. The visual deliverable with annotations.
6. Standout product and interaction improvements.
7. A phased implementation plan and acceptance criteria.
8. A validation plan for the proposed changes.
9. Remaining assumptions, limitations, and decisions requiring my input.

Scale the detail to the product’s complexity. Keep the executive explanation concise and put supporting detail in durable artifacts when appropriate.

Do not assign arbitrary numerical scores. Do not claim the proposal is “the best,” “award-winning,” or guaranteed to improve business metrics.

Do not ask me to approve the audit before conducting it. Complete the authorized inspection and proposal first. Ask for implementation approval only after the recommendation is concrete and reviewable.

If access is partially blocked, finish all independent work and identify exactly what additional access would enable. Do not end with only a list of blockers or an offer to begin.

START NOW

Inspect the product available in this session, identify its core tasks and brand, operate the accessible experience, and deliver the audit, recommended design direction, visual proposal, and implementation plan.
